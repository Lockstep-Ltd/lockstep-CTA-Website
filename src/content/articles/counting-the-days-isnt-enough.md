Quit smoking and, for most people, you put on weight. A meta-analysis of 62 studies found that people who quit without treatment gained an average of 4.67 kg after 12 months, and most of that came within the first three months (Aubin et al., 2012). The common explanation is that the hands and mouth need something to do. Whatever the full mechanism, the pattern is familiar: take one thing away, and something else moves in.

This article makes one argument. When someone quits an addiction, the gap it leaves tends to be filled, and what fills it matters. Yet we usually judge success by one number - how long since they last did it.

## The spiral

Many people will recognise the consequences and chain of events around their habits. For some, they drink, so they gamble. They gamble, so they smoke. Each habit lowers the guard against the next, whilst the loss of control in one place seeps into others.

The research on co-occurrence supports at least part of this. A systematic review of population surveys found that nicotine dependence was the most common co-occurring condition among people experiencing problem gambling, at a mean prevalence of 60.1%, followed by a substance use disorder at 57.5% (Lorains, Cowlishaw and Thomas, 2011). A telling statistic comes from the 2007 British Gambling Prevalence Survey, in which they posit smokers were over three times more likely than non-smokers to experience problem gambling (Griffiths et al., 2010). However, it should be noted that in a more recent British survey, researchers struggled to find conclusive data on whether at-risk gambling was more linked to smoking or risky drinking (Kock et al., 2024). So, whilst we can assume the link is real, it is difficult to be proven given the amount of variables one would have to consider over a larger sample size.

## Does quitting really lead to something else?

In a follow-up of 137 people after residential treatment in South Africa, 36% had substituted their main substance with another substance or behaviour, 23% had relapsed and 40% had stayed abstinent. The substitutes ranged from caffeine and cigarettes to eating, exercise, social media, religion and romantic relationships (Sinclair et al., 2021). So, we can determine whilst substitution and addiction transferability might be prominent, it is overshadowed by the larger two thirds of the sample group who either maintained absolute abstinence or relapsed into their original vice.

Contrarily in gambling specifically, a doctoral thesis reviewing 79 studies found that only 18.18% supported the premise of addiction substitution, while 50.91% supported what the author calls a concurrent model, where people recover from several addictions simultaneously. However, this particular study does not take into account the substitution of practices, specifically, religion. As it is commonly understood that religion often forms the basis for a user’s redemption arc as individuals seek guidance and salvation from a higher power in their darkest times. Whilst that substitution is predominately superior to the individual’s prior vice, it still addresses the same aforementioned gap in focus and energy that this article seeks to address.

## A question about measurement

If the gap is often filled, and the filler can help or harm, then how can we measure success by days abstinent alone? Imagine two people, each a year without a bet. One has rebuilt their evenings around friends, music and sport. The other has moved to substance abuse and has grown further isolated. A day counter scores them the same.

Recovery literature has extrapolated upon this point (although mostly concerning the use of drugs and alcohol). In fact, a study of recovery and health-related quality of life found that years of continuous abstinence explained only 1.5% of the variation in health-related quality of life, and concluded that sobriety alone is ‘not a sufficient measure or proxy of recovery’ (Garner et al., 2014). This differentiation is further exemplified in the conflicting definitions of recovery proposed by academics and government bodies. Whilst the Betty Ford consensus describes a voluntarily maintained lifestyle characterised by *sobriety*, the UK Drug Policy Commission defines it as voluntarily *sustained control of use* (both cited in Best, Vanderplasschen and Nisic, 2020).

## The gap in the literature

Despite their best efforts, the field has not yet answered the question above. Researchers describe the dynamics of substitute behaviours as ‘poorly understood’, note that few studies have looked at prevalence, correlates and motives, and point out that substance-to-behaviour substitution has rarely been examined. Estimates of how common substitution is in treatment samples range from 7% to 92%, partly because there is no universally agreed definition (Sinclair et al., 2021). A scoping review of sex as a substitute behaviour found only 18 peer-reviewed articles published between 1954 and 2023 and called for more qualitative and mixed-methods work, larger samples and stronger designs (Sinclair and Sussman, 2024). Recovery measurement itself is still described as underdeveloped, with no well-defined, multidimensional and psychometrically valid measure (Garner et al., 2014; Best, Vanderplasschen and Nisic, 2020).

## What this means for how we build

This gap changes what we ask of a tool like Lockstep. Blocking and abstinence tracking are often necessary, but they describe only what has stopped. ‘Swap the Night’ starts from the other side: if Friday evenings are where the habit lives, suggest something worth turning up for, such as live music or a comedy night. We don't claim it is proven to prevent substitution. We think the gap deserves a plan, and we'd like to help build the evidence for what works.
